KAZOO CARE

The Kazoo in Care Settings — A Taiwan Study and Nursing Homes Overseas

2026.11.26
Research in progressKAZOO WONDERLAND editorial status

A story we heard — and checked

We had heard that kazoos are used in nursing homes overseas, as both recreation and a form of music therapy. So KAZOO WONDERLAND checked. The story turned out to be true — and there is even a peer-reviewed study behind it.

The Taiwan study — 85 people, a six-month kazoo program

At community care stations in a rural district of southern Taiwan, 85 middle-aged and older adults took part in a six-month kazoo program (Chang et al., Gerontology and Geriatric Medicine, 2023). Participants were divided into a self-learner group and an in-class group, and their pulmonary and cognitive functions were compared before and after.

The self-learner group showed a significant improvement in forced vital capacity (FVC), and maximum expiratory flow (MEF75) improved in both groups. Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores improved significantly in the self-learner group. The researchers conclude that community care stations could consider wind-instrument programs such as the kazoo to support pulmonary function and cognitive reserve.

On the ground in the US — a Kazoo Band and a Kazoo-a-thon

Beyond research, we also found the practice in real facilities. At Spring Gardens, an assisted living and memory care facility in St. George, Utah, residents hold colorful kazoos in a “Kazoo Band” activity featured on the facility's official Instagram. At Westminster Memory Care in Lexington, Kentucky, a “Kazoo-a-thon” event was shared on its official Facebook page. Both are recreation in practice: familiar songs, played together, with a lot of laughter.

Why the kazoo?

The reasons lie in the instrument itself. No technique is needed: if you have a voice, you have a sound, right away. It costs almost nothing, and one kazoo per person is easy to keep hygienic. Above all, the sound itself invites laughter. Not an instrument for “playing well,” but for “making sound together” — that is why the kazoo fits care settings so well.

How to read this article

The Taiwan study is a pilot study — small and preliminary. Its results are encouraging, but they do not prove that the kazoo has a therapeutic effect. On this site we keep what research has shown separate from examples of practice, and we always name our sources. If you know of kazoos being used in care or music therapy, in Japan or anywhere else, we would love to hear from you.
